## Electric Scooter Sharing and Bike Sharing User Behaviour and Characteristics
download: https://www.mdpi.com/2071-1050/12/22/9640<br/>

### Summary

The paper aims to analyze the differences between e-bike users and e-scooter users and to outline their travelling behavior, based on data collected via questionnaires addressed at 633 citizens of Tricity (Poland). The overview proposed by this study is meant to be of help for the creation of new policies, as a successful transport plan, aimed at overcoming urban problems (such as transport costs, traffic congestion, air pollution, space occupied by cars and cars accidents), needs to take into account the characteristics of the groups of people using these transports and their travelling behaviors.

Results show that e-bikes are mainly used as first and last mile transport, as a complementary service and to commute directly, while e-scooters are mostly used for leisure. Indeed, only 30.4% of e-scooter renters used them to get to or from public transport stops, while 51.8% of users rented them just for the fun of the ride. In the context of this usage, the mobility via e-scooters cannot be considered as an effective solution to urban transportation issues.

In Tricity, e-scooters users (56 respondents) are predominantly young adults (31 years old on average, with 16 as minimum and 52 as maximum), on average 3 years younger than e-bikes users, mainly men (62.5%), employed (82.1%), and mostly owning private means of transport (58.9% own a car and 89.3% own a private bike), which suggests that owning a private vehicle is not an obstacle to the diffusion of e-scooters usage. However, younger people not owning a car are more likely to engage in the use of e-scooters, and, most importantly, people that already own a scooter and know how to ride them are more likely to rent them. This is because one of the main reasons for the low user rate of e-scooters is the perceived lack of safety of these vehicles. 
Other potential barriers to the use of e-scooters, reported by the respondent as the main causes discouraging their usage, are road safety concerns, high fees, and low availability. Moreover, people with children do not adopt share mobility services. The study also shows that women use rarely these means of transport if compared with men, even though e-scooters seems to be more easily adopted by women than e-bikes. Regardless of the gender, e-bikes were found to be more popular than e-scooters mostly because they are significantly cheaper.

The study also takes into consideration the advantages of introducing e-cargo bikes, which are thought to be crucial for the European Commission’s goal to “achieve essentially CO₂-free city logistics in major urban centres by 2030”. This kind of service is still rare, although in Tricity e-bikes were sometimes equipped with baskets. The latter was not designed to carry big weights and commercial purposes were not allowed, even though food delivery usage was tolerated. The main barrier to the diffusion of e-cargo bikes seems to be the lack of a standardized regulation.

All in all, e-scooters were significantly less popular than e-bikes, people did not see their usefulness and perceived them as not safe, besides complaining about their high prices. Improving availability and distribution, as well as taking actions to teach people how to properly use these transports may be a good step to grant further adoption of e-scooters.
